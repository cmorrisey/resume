## Q1. Reasons for selecting each project

### P2-REASONS
First Choice: Engineering Project 2, 3D Tissue Engineering Scaffold Fabrication

The obvious fix is a stronger glue. On a premature infant, it is also the wrong one.

That is the problem behind the EMPOWER research project I joined this year: a better adhesive system for the temperature probes on premature infants in the NICU. The incubator heats the baby based on what the probe reads, so a loose probe means the wrong amount of heat. But newborn skin is so thin that a strong adhesive can tear it on removal. The team's prototypes layer silicone, hydrogel or hydrocolloid, foam, and reflective film. In a five-hour movement test, switching from hydrogel to hydrocolloid kept the probe attached after the first design fell off. The material had to hold on and still let go gently. Both.

Tissue engineering scaffolds face a harder version of that problem. A scaffold has to hold cells in place, give them the right pores and stiffness to grow, and then break down as new tissue takes over. Hold on, then let go, inside the body instead of on top of it. I want to learn how engineers tune a material to do that and how they measure whether it worked.

I should be honest about where I stand. I joined the NICU project as its marketing and fundraising lead, so I know its results well enough to pitch them, but I have not run the tests myself yet. This project would put me at the bench. My other work points the same way: on EMPOWER's AbleGamers controller, I help turn user needs into design requirements and test 3D-printed joystick prototypes, and by summer I will have finished Principles of Biomaterials.

Both of my grandparents had pacemakers, and I watched those devices give them years. That is why I chose biomedical engineering. A pacemaker works because its materials can live in the body without harm. A scaffold asks more of a material: to help the body rebuild itself.

### P1-REASONS
Second Choice: Life Sciences Project 1, Integrating Molecular Biology, Genomics, and Gene Therapy to Advance Therapeutic Biomanufacturing

In 2012, contaminated steroid injections from the New England Compounding Center caused a fungal meningitis outbreak that killed more than 60 people. The drug was fine on paper. The process failed.

I learned that history at MeldMD, where I onboard medical spas and clinics to 503A and 503B compounding pharmacies. I assumed memorizing the difference between the two was the easy part of the job, and it was. The harder lesson was why the difference exists. A 503B outsourcing facility is held to federal manufacturing standards because of failures like that one. How a product is made matters as much as what it is.

Gene therapy raises the stakes. When living cells make the product, every batch behaves a little like an experiment, and a small change in the process can change what reaches the patient. This project sits where I want my career to sit: at the point where a therapy that works in a lab becomes a product a hospital can trust every time.

It would also stretch me. My strengths are design, building, and quality control. I spent three summers inspecting work and holding sign-off until every defect was fixed. Molecular biology and genomics are newer to me. I am taking Life: Molecules and Cells now, and this project would put that course to work in a real lab instead of on exams.

I plan to work in the medical device industry, and devices and biologics keep moving closer, from drug-coated stents to cell-based therapies. Both of my grandparents had pacemakers, and neither ever asked how theirs was made. They never had to. That trust is what good manufacturing earns.
